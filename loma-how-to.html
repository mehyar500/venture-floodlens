How to file a LOMA: get FEMA to remove your home from the flood zone

Your mortgage servicer charges you for flood insurance because one letter on FEMA's map says your home is in a high-risk zone. If your house's lowest adjacent grade actually sits at or above the base flood elevation, FEMA's own process — a Letter of Map Amendment (LOMA) — removes your home from the zone. The federal purchase mandate then no longer applies. The filing is free; the surveyor costs a few hundred dollars; the payoff can be $1,021/year in Texas high-risk NFIP premiums (LendingTree, 2025) stopped in their tracks.

The 4 steps, honestly

  1. Find your BFE and zone letter. Run the free FloodLens lookup above (zone letter, in plain English), or pull your property's FIRMette from FEMA's Map Service Center — here's how to read it in 5 minutes. The base flood elevation (BFE) is the number everything hinges on.
  2. Get an elevation certificate from a licensed surveyor. This is the one real cost — typically a few hundred dollars. The surveyor documents your lowest adjacent grade and first-floor elevation against the BFE. If the survey comes back below BFE, stop: a LOMA will not help, and you've only spent the survey fee finding out.
  3. File through FEMA's Online LOMA portal. For a single residential property, the MT-EZ form is the short path — FEMA charges no application fee. Complete applications get a determination in roughly 30–60 days; the most common stall is a missing or wrong elevation certificate, which restarts the clock.
  4. Take the determination letter to your lender. Once FEMA issues the LOMA, your property is officially outside the Special Flood Hazard Area — the federal purchase mandate no longer applies (GAO-09-12). Your servicer still has to drop the forced-placed policy; confirm in writing. Keep your policy anyway if it's cheap: roughly one in three NFIP claims come from outside high-risk zones.

Questions homeowners ask before filing

What is a LOMA?

A Letter of Map Amendment — FEMA's determination that your property's lowest adjacent grade is at or above base flood elevation, so your home is not actually in the Special Flood Hazard Area the map shows. FEMA amends the map for your property only.

How much does a LOMA cost?

FEMA charges nothing for the application itself. The real cost is the elevation certificate: a licensed surveyor typically charges a few hundred dollars. No surveyor, no LOMA — FEMA will not take your word for your elevation.

Does a LOMA remove my flood insurance requirement?

It ends the federal mandate — a federally regulated lender can no longer require flood insurance as a loan condition. Your lender may still want it contractually, and insurers still price elevation. Notify your lender with the determination letter either way.

How long does a LOMA take?

Typically 30–60 days for a complete application, per FEMA's published turnaround. The surveyor step usually takes longest — schedule it first, file second.
