What a flood insurance quote gives you

A quote is an insurer's actual price for actual coverage on your specific property. Under Risk Rating 2.0, it's built from property-level inputs — distance to water, flood type, foundation, first-floor height, rebuilding cost — with private insurers pricing from their own models. A quote costs nothing to request, can turn into a policy that actually pays claims, and is the only number a lender accepts. What it does not give you: context. It's a price without the explanation — why the property prices this way, what levers (elevation certificate, LOMA, private market) might change it, or how your three finalist houses compare.

What a $9 zone report gives you

The FloodLens report answers the question that comes before the quote: "What zone is this address in, and what does that zone typically cost people?" It decodes the FEMA designation into plain English, shows the typical insurance cost range for the zone, explains how premiums are estimated under Risk Rating 2.0, traces how the zone has changed over time, and lists the questions to put to an insurer — a 10-page PDF you can hand to your agent. Two honest limits, stated on every page: it is not insurance and not an official flood determination. The ranges are estimates, not a quote for your property.

The honest math

  • You already have quotes in hand and just need coverage: buy the policy, skip the report. A report adds nothing once an agent has priced your exact property and you're ready to bind.
  • Your lender needs an official determination: that's a flood certification, ordered through the lender's vendor — a consumer report (ours included) is not a substitute.
  • You're comparing houses before offering: this is where the report earns its $9. A report per finalist address (or the $39 3-pack for three) shows which homes carry a flood-cost problem before you're contractually committed.
  • You got a premium that makes no sense: the report explains the mechanics — zone, elevation, Risk Rating 2.0 factors, LOMA path — so the agent conversation starts from "here's what I think is off."

A sensible order of operations

One: run the free lookup on any address you're serious about — the letter alone kills some deals and green-lights others. Two: if the zone raises questions, get the $9 report for the full picture. Three: take the report's questions to two or three agents, including one who sells private flood, and get real quotes. Total spent before the policy itself: $0 to $9 — against a premium that can run several thousand dollars a year, every year you own the home.

Frequently asked questions

Is the FloodLens report an official flood determination?

No. FloodLens reports are informational readings of FEMA's public flood map data. An official flood determination (a flood certification) is produced by specialized vendors for lenders during the loan process, and a LOMA determination comes from FEMA itself. Our report says "not an official flood determination" on every page, because it isn't one.

Is FloodLens flood insurance?

No. FloodLens sells information, not insurance. We don't issue policies, take premiums for coverage, or pay claims. If you need coverage, an NFIP policy through an agent or a private flood insurer is the product that protects you — the report exists to help you shop for it with your eyes open.

Why not just get a free insurance quote instead?

You can — and before binding coverage you should; a quote is the only real price. The report covers the step before: what zone you're in, what it typically costs, and which questions change the quote (elevation certificate, LOMA eligibility, private alternatives). Most useful when comparing properties, or when a premium arrives you can't explain.

When is the $9 report NOT worth it?

If you already hold real quotes and are ready to buy a policy; if your lender needs an official flood certification (a different product, ordered through the lender); or if the free lookup already answered your only question. We'd rather tell you that here than sell you a PDF you don't need.
