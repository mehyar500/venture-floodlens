Questions everyone's asking tonight

What is a Flash Flood Warning?

A Flash Flood Warning is the National Weather Service's most urgent flood alert: flash flooding is ongoing or expected to begin shortly, typically within minutes to hours. It is more severe than a Flood Watch, which means flooding is possible but not yet occurring. If you are in one, the NWS guidance is blunt: "Turn around, don't drown" — most flood deaths happen in vehicles.

Does one flood mean my home is in a FEMA high-risk zone?

No — and the reverse is also true. FEMA flood zones are long-term risk maps, not a record of which streets flooded once. A street outside a mapped high-risk zone can still flood from extreme rainfall, and a street inside one can go decades without water. The zone letter (A, AE, X, …) is what insurers and lenders use to price risk — which is why knowing your official zone matters even if your street "never floods."

Will today's flooding change my flood insurance quote?

Insurers and lenders price off the official FEMA zone map, not the news. One storm doesn't rewrite your zone overnight — but repeated regional flooding is exactly what triggers FEMA map updates, and when a map is redrawn, quotes and lender requirements can change for entire neighborhoods. Check your zone now; that's your baseline before any revision.

After the storm: what your zone means for your insurance

How does my FEMA flood zone change my home insurance quote?

Under FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0, premiums are priced to your property — distance to water, flood type, foundation, first-floor height — not just your zone. But the zone letter still moves real money: in Texas, homes in high-risk zones (A, AE, V, VE) average $1,021/year vs $671/year in low-to-moderate zones — 52% more (published 2025 NFIP averages), and a lender holding a federally backed mortgage requires flood insurance there. And "the same zone" doesn't mean the same price: real 2025 Houston Zone AE premiums span ~$493 to over $4,000, driven by first-floor height vs base flood elevation — which is why the full $9 FloodLens report shows typical cost ranges for your zone and the exact questions to ask your agent. In minimal-risk zones (X, C), coverage is optional and typically cheaper — though roughly one in three NFIP claims comes from outside high-risk areas (FEMA-cited; published estimates range 25–40%).

Could the October 1 flooding raise my premium?

Not overnight — but it can start the chain. Repeated regional flooding feeds FEMA map revisions: when a map is redrawn, whole neighborhoods can move into a higher-risk zone, which changes quotes and can trigger lender flood-insurance requirements on refinance or renewal. It's already happening in Texas: Austin's 2026 FEMA map update is reclassifying some Zone X parcels near Barton, Onion, and Shoal Creeks to Zone AE. Risk Rating 2.0 also factors claim history. Checking your zone now gives you the baseline — before any revision.

My lender never mentioned flood insurance — should I care?

If your home sits in a high-risk zone and your mortgage is federally backed, your lender is required to make you carry flood insurance — and if a future map update pulls your street into one, the requirement can land at renewal or refinance. Knowing your zone letter now means the letter doesn't arrive as a surprise on a bill.
